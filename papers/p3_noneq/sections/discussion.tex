\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Three findings, one arc. The phase map says \emph{where} the interesting
physics lives: not at the calibration anchors but in the wedge between them,
where strategic feedback goes supercritical and response magnitudes stop
being trustworthy. The decoupling result says \emph{what} lives there: the
response layer and the dissipation layer, interchangeable to the eye along
the marginal sweep, are structurally different observables --- a local
derivative and a global flux --- and the near-harmonic regime is where the
difference becomes measurement-relevant. The estimator result says the
dissipation layer is \emph{reachable from data}: rank-exact recovery when the
chain is observed, and a certified lower bound that survives partial
observation, with its tightness itself carrying regime information.

\paragraph{Three things that would have changed our minds, and what happened
when they were tested.} All three of v0.1's open questions have since been run,
and two of them cost us a sentence each.
\emph{(i) Payoff-scale folding.} The $\alpha = 0$ peak at $\lambda \approx 1.7$
sat suspiciously close to scale/2, and the fold is now an exact identity checked
sharply: $\sigma(\lambda, s\cdot u) = \sigma(s\cdot\lambda, u)$ holds to
$0$, so the peak location is a scale statement and not a physical one.
\emph{(ii) The crossover's $\lambda$- and size-dependence.} Measured (F-0010,
F-0022, F-0024): the \emph{collapse} is universal across every $\lambda$ and
action count tested, while the residual \emph{sign} moves with both, so the
robust statement is decorrelation and the reversal is quoted only inside its
scope.
\emph{(iii) The ``suspicious coincidence'' with the supercritical wedge is
\textbf{debunked}, not open.} F-0010's addendum tested it directly on the
per-game data: \textbf{0 of 180 games are supercritical} at
$\lambda\in\{0.8,1.2,2.0\}$, $\alpha=0.95$, consistent with a measured
frontier whose $\lambda_c$ is still above $2$ at $\alpha = 0.95$. The
decoupling and its $\lambda$-dependent sign therefore occur \emph{entirely
outside} the wedge; wedge depth is not the driver, and what looked like a
coincidence was an artefact of the coarse phase map's $\lambda$ range. A pooled
across-$\lambda$ Spearman of $+0.89$ was computed and discarded as
$\lambda$-confounded --- the same confound that produced the marginal-vs-
conditional trap in the first place --- and is recorded so nobody re-runs the
wrong cut. The remaining candidate driver is $\lambda$-dependent equilibrium
concentration, which is a discussion point here and not a claim.

\paragraph{What still would change our minds.} The decoupling's evidence base is
\emph{two-player}: at $N \ge 3$ the meters do not couple at low $\alpha$ in the
first place (F-0023), so the $\alpha$-stratified design that produced every
number in this section cannot be run there, and a mechanism that predicted
coupling at $N \ge 3$ would not be contradicted by anything we have measured.
The sector-loading test named in F-0023 --- regress per-game $\R$ and $\EPR$ on
the per-sector harmonic norms at $N = 3$ --- is the one pass over existing data
that would settle why.

\paragraph{Toward data.} The empirical programme (Stage 3 of the repository)
points these meters at systems whose $\alpha$ nobody knows: retail pricing
panels and electricity bidding. The division of labour established here is
what makes that honest: the reciprocity test for potentiality (exact,
$\lambda$-free as a symmetry statement), the TUR bound for dissipation
(certified, partial-observation-tolerant), and neither doing the other's job.
The nearest empirical precedent is the entropy-production detection of an
Edgeworth price cycle in experimental data \citep{xu2011}; the instruments
here are built to make such readings routine, calibrated, and falsifiable.
